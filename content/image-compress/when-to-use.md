## Common Scenarios for Image Compression

### Speeding Up a Website

Large images are the most common reason websites load slowly. Visitors leave pages that take more than a few seconds to appear, and search engines rank faster sites higher. Compressing product photos, blog images, and background graphics before uploading them can cut page load times dramatically without any visible change in quality.

### Sending Photos by Email

Most email providers cap attachments at 10-25 MB. A few uncompressed photos from a modern phone camera can blow past that limit. Compressing each image to 60-80% quality typically reduces file size by half or more, letting you attach several photos in a single email without delivery failures.

### Meeting Upload Size Limits

Online forms, job applications, and government portals often require images under a specific file size — commonly 1 MB or 2 MB. If your photo or scan exceeds the limit, compression brings it within range without needing to crop or resize the image itself.

### Freeing Up Device Storage

Phones and laptops with limited storage fill up faster than expected, especially with photos. Compressing older images you want to keep but don't need at full quality can reclaim gigabytes of space over a large photo library — all without deleting anything.

### Preparing Images for Social Media

Social media platforms compress uploaded images on their own, often aggressively. Starting with a thoughtfully compressed image gives you more control over the final result. Setting quality to around 80% typically produces a file that looks clean after the platform applies its own processing, rather than being double-compressed into a muddy version.
